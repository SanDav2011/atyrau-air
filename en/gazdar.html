About gases

What the stations measure, how they measure it and how it affects health

Stations continuously measure gases and dust in the air with automatic instruments and report a value every hour. Below, for each substance: how it is measured, how it affects health and what is noticeable at which level.

Units. This site uses µg/m³: the mass of a substance in one cubic metre of air, in micrograms (1000 µg/m³ = 1 mg/m³). Foreign texts, especially workplace limits, often use ppm: parts of gas per million parts of air. Conversion depends on the gas: for example, for H2S 1 ppm ≈ 1400 µg/m³, so 0.003 ppm ≈ 4 µg/m³. To avoid confusion, the limit values below are given in both units (25 °C).

Limit values follow Order No. ҚР ДСМ-70 (the maximum one-time limit is for 20–30 minutes). A station gives an hourly average, so the comparison is approximate.

H2SSO2NO2NOCOO3NH3PM

H2S — hydrogen sulfide

The “rotten egg” smell

What is it?
A colourless gas. Sources: oil refining, oil and gas fields, treatment plants, evaporation ponds, swamps.
How it is measured
Usually by ultraviolet fluorescence: SO2 in the air is first filtered out, H2S is heated and converted to SO2, and its glow under ultraviolet light is measured. Some stations may have a cheaper electrochemical sensor, which is less accurate. The exact instrument used at the Atyrau stations has not been confirmed yet.
Limit value
8 µg/m³ (≈0.006 ppm), one-time (ҚР ДСМ-70).
What happens at different levels
0.2–2 µg/m³Sensitive people start to notice the smell; at 0.6–6 µg/m³ they start to recognise it (WHO).
1–10 µg/m³At our stations: usually 1, at most 10 (14–19 October 2026).
7 µg/m³If it stays above this for 30 minutes, complaints about the smell become more frequent (WHO).
150 µg/m³Over 24 hours — a limit based on eye irritation (WHO).
≈14,000 µg/m³
10 ppm		With prolonged exposure — eye irritation, headache, fatigue (ATSDR).
≈140,000 µg/m³
100 ppm		The sense of smell is lost — the smell no longer warns you. Immediately dangerous to life (NIOSH).
The level at our stations is a level at which the smell is noticeable; it is thousands of times lower than the harmful levels given by ATSDR.
For comparison — workplace limits
For healthy adult workers over an 8-hour shift, not for residents:
  • Canadian provinces: 10 ppm (≈14,000 µg/m³) for 8 hours; ACGIH (Ontario): 1 ppm (≈1400 µg/m³).
  • NIOSH (USA): 10 ppm for 10 minutes; 100 ppm is a level immediately dangerous to life.
The Kazakhstan limit for residents (8 µg/m³) is about 175 times lower than the strictest workplace limit: residents include children, the elderly and the sick, and they breathe this air around the clock.
Who is more sensitive
People with asthma — in some, breathing becomes difficult, with possible headache and nausea (ATSDR). There is little research on children.
What you can do
If the smell is strong — close the windows and postpone a long walk.

SO2 — sulfur dioxide

A gas with a sharp smell

What is it?
A colourless gas with a sharp smell. Sources: burning fuel that contains sulfur, oil refining, CHP plants.
How it is measured
Ultraviolet fluorescence (the reference method in the USA).
Limit value
500 µg/m³ (≈0.19 ppm) one-time, 50 µg/m³ (≈0.019 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70). WHO guideline: 40 µg/m³ per day.
Effects
Irritates the airways and makes breathing harder even after short exposure (EPA).
Who is more sensitive
People with asthma, especially children (EPA).
At our stations
Usually 2 µg/m³, sometimes rising to 70–150 (14–19 October 2026).

NO2 — nitrogen dioxide

Formed during combustion

What is it?
A brown gas. Sources: traffic, CHP plants, combustion in industry.
How it is measured
Chemiluminescence: the light emitted when NO reacts with ozone is measured; NO2 is first converted to NO.
Limit value
200 µg/m³ (≈0.11 ppm) one-time, 40 µg/m³ (≈0.021 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70). WHO (2021): 25 per day, 10 per year.
Effects
Irritates the airways. In the short term — cough, wheezing, shortness of breath, especially in asthma. In the long term — it may contribute to developing asthma and increases susceptibility to respiratory infections (EPA).
Who is more sensitive
People with asthma, children, the elderly (EPA).
At our stations
Usually 5 µg/m³, sometimes up to 70–150 (14–19 October 2026).

NO — nitric oxide

Quickly turns into NO2 in the air

What is it?
A colourless gas formed during combustion; it quickly turns into NO2 in the air.
How it is measured
Chemiluminescence (in the same instrument as NO2).
Limit value
400 µg/m³ (≈0.33 ppm) one-time, 60 µg/m³ (≈0.049 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70).
Effects
Because NO quickly turns into NO2, the health effects are assessed mainly through NO2 — see the NO2 section.

CO — carbon monoxide

Colourless and odourless, even though it is popularly called “the smelly gas”

What is it?
A colourless, odourless gas — so people cannot sense it. Sources: traffic, combustion, faulty stoves and gas heaters.
How it is measured
Infrared absorption (NDIR).
Limit value
5000 µg/m³ (≈4.4 ppm) one-time, 3000 µg/m³ (≈2.6 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70). WHO: 4000 per day.
Effects
Less oxygen reaches the organs (heart, brain) in the blood. At raised outdoor levels, people with heart disease may have chest pain (angina), especially during exertion. At very high levels — dizziness, confusion, loss of consciousness; such levels occur mostly indoors, not outdoors (EPA).
Who is more sensitive
People with heart disease (EPA).

O3 — ozone (ground-level)

Formed in sunlight

What is it?
It is not emitted directly — it forms in sunlight from NO2 and other substances. It increases in the daytime heat.
How it is measured
Ultraviolet photometry — ozone absorbs ultraviolet light.
Limit value
160 µg/m³ (≈0.082 ppm) one-time, 30 µg/m³ (≈0.015 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70). WHO: 100 over 8 hours.
Effects
Cough, sore throat, difficulty breathing deeply, pain when inhaling; inflammation of the airways (EPA).
Who is more sensitive
People with asthma, children, the elderly, people who spend a lot of time outdoors (EPA).

NH3 — ammonia

A gas with a sharp smell

What is it?
A colourless gas with a sharp smell. Sources: fertilisers, livestock farms, some industries.
How it is measured
The method used at the Atyrau stations has not been confirmed yet.
Limit value
200 µg/m³ (≈0.29 ppm) one-time, 40 µg/m³ (≈0.057 ppm) daily (ҚР ДСМ-70).
Effects
No harm has been found at ordinary environmental levels. At high levels the eyes, throat and lungs are irritated and there is coughing (ATSDR).
Who is more sensitive
Some people with asthma (ATSDR).

PM10 and PM2.5 — dust particles

Not a gas — tiny particles in the air

What is it?
PM10 are particles smaller than 10 µm in diameter, PM2.5 smaller than 2.5 µm (about 30 times thinner than a hair). Sources: combustion, traffic, construction, dust lifted by the wind.
How it is measured
Usually by beta attenuation (how much the dust collected on a filter weakens the radiation) or an optical method (light scattering; cheap, but less accurate at high humidity). The reference method is weighing the filter. The exact instrument used at the Atyrau stations has not been confirmed yet.
Limit value
PM10 — 300 µg/m³ one-time, 60 daily; PM2.5 — 160 one-time, 35 daily (ҚР ДСМ-70). WHO: PM2.5 — 15 per day, 5 per year; PM10 — 45 per day, 15 per year. ppm is not used for particles.
Effects
Particles penetrate deep into the lungs and sometimes into the blood. Irritation of the airways, cough, difficulty breathing, worsening asthma, heart rhythm disturbances; people with heart and lung disease face a higher risk of premature death (EPA). WHO: cardiovascular and respiratory disease, cancer.
Who is more sensitive
People with heart or lung disease, children, the elderly (EPA).
This is not medical advice. The levels and symptoms come from official sources. If you feel worse, call 103 or see a doctor.
